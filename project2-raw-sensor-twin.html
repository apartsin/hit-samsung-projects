RAW Sensor Digital Twin

A neural model that predicts the exact RAW mosaic a given camera sensor would record, before demosaicing, from an input image, the capture conditions, and the sensor and lens parameters.

A research project for an industry customer. There is no ready recipe: the team defines the details of the method, reads the literature, tries ideas, measures, and learns from what does not work. The project requires independent work, proactive exploration and a lot of trial and error, with regular reporting to the supervisor and the Samsung mentor.

Description

A camera sensor does not record a color image. Each pixel sits under one color filter (red, green or blue, in a Bayer pattern) and counts photons, adding noise from the physics of light and from the electronics. The camera's image signal processor (ISP) later turns this RAW mosaic into the photo we see.

Students build a digital twin of this first stage: a model that answers "what would this sensor have recorded?" for a scene, under given settings, before any processing.

Input: an image of the scene (sRGB or linear), plus

• Capture conditions: exposure time, ISO (analog gain), f-number, focal length, illuminant and color temperature, scene brightness.

• Sensor parameters: CFA pattern, pixel pitch, spectral sensitivity, full-well capacity, read noise, dark current, black level, bit depth.

• Optics parameters: lens blur (PSF or MTF), vignetting, chromatic aberration.

Output: a single-channel RAW Bayer mosaic with realistic signal, clipping, quantization and noise, matching what the real sensor produces. Each pixel holds one number (typically 10 to 14 bits), the light measured through its own red, green or blue filter; the other two colors at that pixel do not exist yet and are only filled in later by demosaicing.

The project combines two worlds: a physics model of the camera (light falling off at the edges, the response of each color channel, shot and read noise, clipping) and a learned model that corrects what the physics misses. The physics part keeps the output plausible for unseen settings; the learned part makes it match real data.

Motivation

  • Training data for camera AI. Denoising, demosaicing and low-light models need large amounts of RAW data from a specific sensor. Capturing it is slow and expensive; a twin can generate it from ordinary photos.
  • Designing the next sensor. Engineers can ask "how would images look with smaller pixels, more read noise, or a different color filter?" before the silicon exists.
  • Testing the ISP. A twin lets the full camera pipeline be tested on thousands of virtual captures with controlled conditions.
  • Current research. Models that convert RGB back to RAW using camera settings, such as ParamISP (CVPR 2024, with Samsung co-authors), show the field is active and has open questions.

Goals and deliverables

  1. A physics-based simulator in PyTorch: inverse tone and color processing, lens effects, spectral to CFA mapping, signal-dependent noise, clipping and quantization, all controlled by parameters.
  2. Sensor calibration: noise parameters (per ISO) estimated from public data or from flat-field and dark frames, following the EMVA 1288 method.
  3. A learned twin: a network conditioned on the capture and sensor parameters that refines the simulator output (or replaces part of it) to match real RAW captures.
  4. An evaluation on held-out real RAW images: pixel accuracy, noise statistics, and a downstream test (a denoiser trained on twin data vs. real data).
  5. Optional: a small "what-if" tool that shows how one scene changes as sensor parameters change.

Suggested approach

  • Start from Unprocessing (Brooks et al.): invert tone mapping, gamma, color correction and white balance to get linear RGB, then mosaic it.
  • Add physics: lens vignetting and blur, exposure scaling from exposure time, ISO and f-number, then a Poisson-Gaussian noise model (shot noise plus read noise), and later the richer ELD model (row noise, quantization, dark current).
  • Condition a network (U-Net with FiLM conditioning or a small transformer) on the parameter vector, as ParamISP does, to learn the residual between simulated and real RAW.
  • Learn the noise with a generative model (normalizing flow as in Noise Flow, or diffusion as in RAW-Diffusion and NoiseDiff) so that the noise distribution matches, not just its mean.
  • Train and test across ISO and exposure so the twin works at settings that were not in the training set.

What students will need

A map of the concepts, methods and tools this project touches. The first weeks are for learning the essentials.

Concepts

  • Image formation: photons, quantum efficiency, exposure
  • CMOS sensor operation, Bayer CFA, quad Bayer
  • The camera ISP: black level, white balance, demosaicing, color matrix, tone curve, gamma
  • Linear vs. display color spaces, spectral sensitivity
  • Noise sources: shot, read, dark current, row noise, quantization
  • Optics: PSF, MTF, vignetting, chromatic aberration
  • EXIF and DNG metadata

Algorithms and techniques

  • Inverse ISP (unprocessing)
  • Poisson-Gaussian and physics-based noise models
  • Noise calibration: photon transfer curve, noise level function
  • Conditional networks (FiLM, parameter embeddings)
  • Generative modeling of noise: normalizing flows, diffusion
  • Differentiable simulation, hybrid physics + learning
  • Image alignment for paired data

Models

  • U-Net, residual CNNs
  • CycleISP, InvISP, ParamISP (reverse ISP)
  • Noise Flow, ELD, LRD (noise models)
  • RAW-Diffusion (generative RAW)
  • Denoisers for the downstream test (e.g. U-Net from SID)

Evaluation

  • PSNR and SSIM in the RAW domain
  • KL divergence of noise histograms per ISO and channel
  • Noise level function and photon transfer curve match
  • Downstream test: denoiser trained on twin vs. real data

Tools and frameworks

  • Python, PyTorch, NumPy
  • rawpy (LibRaw) for reading RAW and DNG
  • colour-science, colour-demosaicing
  • kornia rgb_to_raw / raw_to_rgb
  • simple-camera-pipeline (SIDD rendering)
  • ISETCam (MATLAB) as a reference simulator
  • exiftool, Git, Weights & Biases

Prerequisites

  • Required: prior knowledge of deep learning and PyTorch, from a completed course or a significant project (training a network, writing a Dataset and DataLoader, a training loop).
  • Required: good Python and NumPy.
  • Recommended: a course in image processing or signal processing; basic probability (noise distributions). Interest in physics and cameras helps.

Compute: a GPU with 12 GB or more.
